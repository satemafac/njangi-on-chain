> For over a year I've been building something I haven't said much about. Today I'd like to introduce it. I'm the founder and CEO of Njangi On-Chain.
>
> Every month for as long as I can remember, my family has run a njangi.
>
> If you grew up Cameroonian you know the word; elsewhere it's a tontine, a susu, a chama, a stokvel. Everyone puts in the same amount, one person takes the whole pot, and next month it's someone else's turn until everyone has had one. No bank. It has worked for centuries, for people the banks were never built for. It's the oldest financial system I know, and it runs on one thing: trust.
>
> I love it. I'm also in it, so I've seen where it strains. One person holds everyone's cash. One notebook is the only record. When memory and the notebook disagree, someone gets blamed, usually the aunt who did nothing wrong. And once a family is spread across Douala, Maryland and Paris, the circle that held us together gets hard to keep.
>
> So I started building the answer.
>
> I'd spent years following the crypto and financial world, long enough to believe the new rails are better for people like us: faster, cheaper, and not built to leave anyone at the door. What I hadn't found was a community building for the people I had in mind, until I found @Sui Foundation. Their goal was the next billion users, and they had the means to mean it: zkLogin, which lets someone sign in with the Google account they already have and no seed phrase to lose; sponsored transactions, so a member never has to buy a token to pay their own contribution; and Move, a language a developer can pick up in a weekend. When Sui ran a hackathon, I built the first version of Njangi On-Chain in it. To @Mysten Labs and Sui, who built those rails: thank you.
>
> What it is today: a management platform for the people who run these circles. The rotation handled, reminders sent where the circle already talks, and a record every member can check. Contributions held in escrow each cycle where no company can touch them, released only to the member whose turn it is, in Douala or Dallas. There is no function in the system that lets us move a circle's money. Even recovering a circle's money takes a vote of its members. Organisers never carry anyone's cash again, or the blame that comes with it.
>
> Where it goes next: the Circle Record. Two decades of paying in on time earns most members nothing they can show anyone. This is proof of participation the member owns and chooses who to show, including a bank. Not a score we compute about you. A record you hold. Then circles that govern themselves, rule changes put to a member vote, and a network of circles that can vouch for one another. That's the whole idea: a community spread across the world, building on the system it has always trusted.
>
> If your family runs one of these, I'd like to hear how it works today.
>
> Njangi On-Chain

> On Tuesday the Senate voted 49 to 50 on whether to advance the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act. It needed 60. The bill is stalled, and by its own sponsor's account, done for this session.
>
> We build coordination software for savings circles, and nothing in that bill was about a family njangi. But we followed every line of the debate, because the question underneath it is the one we built around: who holds the money, and what happens to it when they have a bad year.
>
> Here is why the week leaves us more optimistic, not less.
>
> The bill did not fail on its substance. It failed on ethics language about officials' own holdings. The market-structure framework itself, which regulator watches which market, cleared committee with votes from both parties back in May.
>
> The regulators are not waiting. SEC Chair Paul Atkins said this week that "with or without legislation, we will act decisively within the SEC's statutory authority." CFTC Chair Mike Selig said his agency is "locked in and ready to ship its rules for the new frontier of finance." The SEC's proposed Regulation Crypto Assets is open for public comment now.
>
> And none of it changes what we do. Nobody holds the pot. Not the organiser, and not us. There is no function in our system that lets us move a circle's money, we take no cut of anyone's pot, and we pay nothing on balances because we never hold one. That design was never a bet on a bill. It is the lesson of every consumer-savings failure of the last twenty years: the trouble starts when somebody in the middle has a capability they should never have had.
>
> Clear rules are good for people who build with nothing to hide. We will keep building, and we will keep reading.
>
